What these bands become in March

November ranks are not March seeds.

The committee seeds the field on the whole season, and four months of games will shuffle this list.

But the bands rhyme with the seed lines, and the seed lines carry 41 years of counted history (the full analysis).

Read today's list in bands:

  • Ranks 1 to 4 (Florida, Duke, Illinois, UConn): the 1-seed line. One seeds have won 27 of 41 titles and 162 of their 164 first games. The safest seat in sports, with two famous exceptions.
  • Ranks 5 to 8 (Michigan State, Virginia, Texas, Houston): the 2-seed line. Strong, not safe: 15 seeds have beaten 2 seeds 11 times, about once every four years.
  • Ranks 9 to 12 (UCLA, Louisville, Tennessee, Arizona): the 3-seed line. Three seeds lose to 14 seeds about 14 times in 100.
  • Ranks 13 to 16 (St. John's, Gonzaga, Kansas, Texas Tech): the 4-seed line. The first bullseye. Four seeds lose to 13 seeds 33 times in 164, about one time in five.
  • Ranks 17 to 20 (Miami, Alabama, Arkansas, Michigan): the 5-seed line. The most famous trap in the tournament. Five seeds lose to 12 seeds 58 times in 164, better than one time in three. And no 5 seed has ever won the title. The reigning champion, Michigan, sits in this band today.
  • Ranks 21 to 25 (USC, Vanderbilt, BYU, Kentucky, Nebraska): the 6 and 7 lines. Favorites in the two most upset-heavy games after the coin flip: 6 seeds lose to 11 seeds about 39 times in 100, and 7 seeds lose to 10 seeds about 38 times in 100.

    Where the model rates a ranked team far below the voters, one of the two lists is wrong.

    Cinderella Radar: unranked teams that could surprise in March

    March's 11, 12, and 13 seeds are almost never ranked in November.

    They are conference champions and fighters from leagues the polls barely watch.

    Count the whole first round and about eight games a year go to the lower seed. About two double-digit seeds are still standing in the Sweet 16.

    So this page scores the unranked names on three signals. All three are public, and you can check every one:

    • Model strength. Torvik's model rates the team in the national top 70 even though the voters left it out. Teams that play above their seed line are where upsets come from.
    • Poll notice. The team received AP votes. At least a few voters thought it belonged, which separates it from the 300 or so teams nobody mentioned.
    • A live path. Its league's road to March lands teams on the 10 to 14 seed lines, where the counted upset rates run from about 14 to 39 in 100.

    What this page cannot see

    Plain words about the limits, because a forecast that hides them is a sales pitch:

    • One model, named every time. Torvik's T-Rank is free, public, and on the committee's own team sheets. Other respected ratings sit behind paywalls, or publish nothing in November that can be checked. One model you can verify beats three we cannot.
    • Preseason means priors. In November, the model is rating last season's program, returning players, and history, not games this roster has played. Its disagreements with the voters are hypotheses, and this page treats them that way.
    • No injuries, no roster news. This page does not track who is hurt or who transferred. By February, that will matter more than anything printed here, and the weekly refresh will say what changed.
    • A rank is not a seed. The bands rhyme with the seed lines. The committee holds the real pen, in March.

    Common questions

    Are the November rankings the same as the March seeds?

    No. The committee seeds the tournament on the whole season in March. November ranks are a starting picture. The bands rhyme with the seed lines, which is why this page reads them that way, but any team can climb or fall by March.

    When does this page update?

    Every week of the season. The AP poll lands on Mondays once games begin, and this page refreshes with it, with the date and source stamped at the top. Every refresh is saved as a dated snapshot, and the trend column reads from those snapshots: week one honestly read "First snapshot," and from Nov. 9 on it shows real movement between dated polls.

    Who is the defending champion?

    Michigan, which won the April 2026 title game. The Wolverines sit at No. 20 in the Nov. 9, 2026 AP poll after losing to UConn.

    Which unranked teams could surprise in March?

    A list of unranked teams picked out using three public signals: a top-70 rating in Bart Torvik's T-Rank model, votes in the AP poll, and a league path that lands teams on the 10 to 14 seed lines. It is a list to watch, not a prediction that any of them makes the field.

    Why does each Top 25 rank have a small second number?

    The big number is the team's AP rank. The small raised number is its rank in Bart Torvik's T-Rank model, out of 365 teams, read on November 1, 2026. Putting both in one cell shows where the voters and the model agree, and where they do not, with no tap or second table needed.

    What makes a favorite 'vulnerable' on this page?

    A gap: the model rates the team eight or more spots below the voters. One of the two lists is wrong. If it is the voters, that team arrives in March standing on a trap seed line with less under it than its rank suggests. The list is re-scored every week.
